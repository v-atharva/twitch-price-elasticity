Analysis: Variable Subscription Pricing

Did cheaper subs sell more subs?

The natural intuition is that, lowering the cost of a subscription would tend to attract more new subscribers. This hypothesis is not just tested but quantified in this project

TL;DR version

More subscribers, when each paying significantly less, the revenue generated from the subscriptions comes out about even.

01

Context

1 History

Subscribing to a Twitch channel cost about $4.99, irrespective of the region.

  • In 2021, Twitch proposedTwitch Blog · Aug 5, 2021Local sub pricing expands worldwide — Twitch's announcement of the country-by-country pricing rollout. a new model: adjust subscription prices by each country's purchasing power and local currency.
  • The rollout was phased and was done to match the:
    • PPP of the individuals
    • Currency of the country.
    • This resulted in different countries getting different discounts on different dates.

was

That messy rollout is the whole opportunity. Three properties turn it into something we can measure:

  • The timing was staggered. Turkey and Mexico in May, Latin America in July, Europe and Asia-Pacific in August. Countries that had not been reached yet act as a stand-in for what the treated ones would have done.
  • The discounts differed in size. Turkey lost 77% of its price, Spain only 20%. If price is what moves subscriptions, deeper cuts should produce bigger jumps — a prediction we can test, not just a direction.
  • The US price never moved. That leaves a group of channels that lived through the same year, the same games and the same platform trends, but never got a discount.

None of this was our choice, which is the point. We are not running an experiment on Twitch; we are reading one Twitch ran on itself.

the technical version

The three properties above are exactly the ingredients a modern staggered difference-in-differences design needs: variation in treatment timing across cohorts, a continuous dose that varies across those cohorts, and a never-treated control group.

The dose is defined as the log price ratio at rollout, log(new price / old price), evaluated at the ECB exchange rate on the rollout date. Because Twitch set the new price in local currency, the local-currency ratio is itself FX-free — the exchange rate only matters for expressing doses on a common scale.

Cohorts as estimated: TR and MX (May 20), BR and AR (Jul 27), ES (Aug 5). Doses run from −1.44 log points in Turkey to −0.22 in Spain.

Step 1

Same product, many prices, on a schedule Twitch set rather than one we picked. Now we need a fair way to compare.

02

The method: a fair comparison

2 How we isolate the price effect

Subscriptions rise and fall for a hundred reasons. How do we separate the price from everything else?

We compare channels whose audiences got a discount against channels whose audiences did not, over the same months. A Turkish-language channel gets Turkey's discount. A US-focused channel never does. Take the change in the first group, subtract the change in the second, and anything that hit both (a good streaming year, a viral game) cancels out.

What is left is the price effect. Economists call this difference-in-differences, and it has been the workhorse of causal measurement for decades.

The control group: US-audience channels, the one market where the price never moved. Their few discounted international viewers only make our estimate conservative.

The idea is simple. Making it hold up took four decisions, each of which could have quietly broken the answer.

  • Who counts as treated? The prices changed for viewers, by country. But subscriptions are counted per channel, and a channel does not have a country — it has an audience. We assign each channel to the country of its dominant audience, read off broadcast language plus where the streamer is based.
    • Spanish is split rather than pooled: Spain, Mexico and Argentina got different discounts on different dates, so treating them as one group would blur three experiments into one.
  • Which estimator? The textbook approach to this kind of comparison quietly uses channels that were already discounted as a stand-in for ones about to be. Since those channels are already responding to their own price cut, they make a poor "before" picture. Running it anyway gives +0.84; the estimator built for staggered rollouts gives +0.56. We report the second and show the arithmetic of the first.
  • When does treatment start? Twitch announced the change on May 17 and switched Turkey on May 20 — so the month before is not clean, and neither is a month a rollout landed in the middle of. We let effects begin a month early rather than pretend the boundary is sharp, and only count a month as treated if it contains at least 15 treated days.
  • How do we get a price sensitivity out of this? In two steps rather than one. First measure each country's effect on its own terms. Then line those five effects up against the five discount sizes and read the slope. A single pooled regression would have hidden whether the dose-response pattern exists at all — and that pattern is the actual evidence that we are measuring price rather than a coincidence.
the technical version
  • Estimator. Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) group-time ATTs, doubly robust, never-treated controls, anticipation = 1. Cohort assignment by broadcast language + streamer country; es split into ES / MX / AR.
  • Why not TWFE. Two-way fixed effects on the identical panel returns 0.84 against 0.56 for CS. A Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition names the culprit: the "forbidden" later-vs-earlier comparisons average 0.11 while clean treated-vs-never comparisons average 0.43. TWFE averages the two together with weights nobody chose.
  • Two-stage elasticity. Stage one yields a mature-window ATT (months 6–12 post) per cohort. Stage two regresses those ATTs on each cohort's log price change by WLS through the origin — the intercept is restricted because a zero price change must imply a zero effect. The slope is the elasticity.
  • Inference. With 29 treated channels, analytic standard errors are optimistic. The quotable uncertainty is a channel-level cluster bootstrap (170 effective draws) and a 200-draw permutation test, not the analytic SE.

More subscribers is not the same as more money. Each new subscriber paid a heavily discounted price. So what happened to revenue? For that we went past the tracker, to Twitch's own records.

Cross-checked against Twitch's own books

More subscribers. Not more money.

Our subscriber counts come from a public tracker, so we matched all 68 channels against Twitch's internal payout records from 2021, handled privately and never published. Everything lined up.

channels found in Twitch's records68 / 68

That last line is the story. Far more subscribers, each paying far less, adds up to about the same revenue. It is likely why Twitch paired the rollout with a 12-month revenue guarantee for streamers.

verified

It is worth being precise about why that flat revenue line is more than a footnote. Flat revenue is the arithmetic fingerprint of the number we estimated. A price sensitivity just under 1 means volume rises by slightly less, in percentage terms, than price falls — so the two nearly cancel and revenue lands close to where it started. Had the true sensitivity been well above 1, revenue would have visibly risen; well below 1, it would have visibly fallen.

So the payout records do not merely confirm that our subscriber counts are real. They reproduce the headline finding through a completely different quantity, from a completely different source — Twitch's own books rather than a public tracker.

the technical version
  • Elasticity. −0.90, analytic SE 0.098, cluster-bootstrap 95% CI [−1.36, −0.34]. The CI is what to quote; it is wide because 29 treated channels is a small sample, and the design does not pretend otherwise.
  • Overall ATT. +0.56 log points (SE 0.21) on paid subscriber counts — roughly +75% against comparable US channels.
  • The revenue rerun. The identical CS specification, with subscription revenue from the payout records swapped in as the outcome, gives an ATT of −0.06 (SE 0.17) — statistically indistinguishable from zero, while counts rose ~75%. That is the signature of |ε| < 1.
  • Count validity. All 68 panel channels appear in the payout records. Log scraped counts correlate with log actual revenue at r = 0.73 pooled (0.49 median within-channel), and implied revenue per active subscriber is ~$2.80 — about the 50% a streamer keeps of a $4.99 sub. The counts are correctly scaled, and a difference-in-differences design is robust to the level noise that remains.
  • Ethics boundary. The 2021 payout leak is breach-obtained personal financial data. It was handled locally only; the validation module takes an owner-supplied path and no-ops without it, so nothing from it is committed, published, or required to reproduce the study.

04

The checks: could this be wrong?

4 Trying to break our own result

Six checks, each aimed at a different way this could have been a mirage. All six either passed or left the effect smaller but clearly present.

The free-subs check
passed

Prime subs cost nothing, so a price cut should not move them. It did not.

The fluke check
1 in 200

We shuffled which channels got the cut 200 times, and no shuffle matched the real effect.

The head-start check
passed

Treated channels were not already pulling ahead before their prices changed.

The method check
−50% inflation

The simpler textbook estimator overstates the effect by half, so we used one built for staggered rollouts.

The worst-case check
still positive

Assume the tiny pre-existing drift had continued, and the effect shrinks by half but stays.

The dry-run check
exact recovery

The full pipeline first ran on simulated data with a planted answer, and recovered it.

the check that failed

One of these tests failed first — and that is why the design looks the way it does.

Prime subs come bundled free with Amazon Prime. Nobody pays for them, so a price cut has nothing to act on. If our method reports that free subscriptions surged when prices fell, the method is picking up something other than price.

An early version of this analysis did exactly that. Prime subs "responded" +1.07 — a huge effect on a product with no price. What it had actually found was the 2021 Spanish-language Twitch boom: those channels were growing for reasons that had nothing to do with what a subscription cost, and the specification was crediting that growth to the discount.

That failure is what produced two of the decisions in step 2 — letting effects begin before the rollout date, and measuring each country against its own discount size instead of pooling them. Under the final specification the same test returns −0.07, which is nothing.

+1.07 early specification → −0.07 final specification

One more result is worth pulling out of the robustness table, because it is a finding rather than a check. Splitting the treated channels by size, the entire effect sits with the smaller ones: below-median channels gained +0.90, while the largest channels showed essentially nothing at +0.02.

That is what you would expect if affordability is the mechanism — a discount matters where price was the obstacle, and the biggest channels' audiences were subscribing regardless. It also implies our headline number is a floor. This sample can only see large creators, and the response looks stronger the further down the size distribution you go.

the technical version
  • Prime placebo. Final specification: −0.07 (SE 0.22). Superseded early specification: +1.07, driven by the Spanish/Brazilian popularity boom — documented as design history, not a current output.
  • Fake treatment dates assigned to never-treated US channels: −0.23 (SE 0.27), comfortably covering zero.
  • Permutation inference. 200 random reassignments of treated status. The observed ATT of +0.56 exceeds every permuted draw (2.5–97.5% of the null spans −0.36 to +0.48), giving p = 0.005 — the strongest statement 29 treated units support.
  • Pre-trends. Joint test on 5 leads: p = 0.35, no evidence against parallel trends. But honest-DiD trend sensitivity is the caveat that matters: an insignificant lead slope of +0.03/month, if extrapolated through the post period, would pull the post-period average from 0.43 down to 0.21. The effect stays positive under that assumption; its magnitude does not survive intact. Reported rather than buried.
  • Robustness span. Not-yet-treated controls give an identical 0.56. Drop-one-country ranges 0.50–0.65. Language-only assignment (Spanish pooled — a deliberate misassignment stress test) gives 0.55; winsorizing at 1/99 within month gives 0.59.
  • Heterogeneity. Below-median pre-period size +0.90 (SE 0.29) against above-median +0.02 (SE 0.27).
Step 4

Six ways it could have been a mirage, six checks — and one earlier failure that rebuilt the design. The finding held.

05

The build: how do you trust the pipeline?

5 Checking the instrument before the measurement

The checks in step 4 test the finding. But what tests the code that produced it?

A subtle bug in an estimation pipeline does not announce itself. It returns a plausible number, and a plausible number is indistinguishable from a correct one. Three things were done about that, all before the real data was touched.

01 · known answer

Test the ruler on a known length

Before running on Twitch data, the pipeline ran on simulated data with the answer planted in it — a fake panel built with the real cohort structure and a price sensitivity we chose. If the code cannot recover a number we put there ourselves, it cannot be trusted with one we don't know.

02 · second opinion

Two languages, same answer

The estimator exists as libraries in both Python and R, written by different people. Both run on the same data and their results are required to match, which rules out trusting a single library's bugs.

03 · sensitive data

The leaked records never enter the repo

The payout cross-check reads a file supplied from outside the project and skips itself entirely when that file is absent. Anyone can clone and reproduce the study; nobody inherits breach-obtained financial data by doing so.

the technical version
  • Estimator validation. make synth && make test generates a synthetic panel carrying the real cohort structure and a planted elasticity of −0.6. CI asserts that the CS event study recovers the planted dynamic effects, that the recovered elasticity lands within −0.60 ± 0.05 (it returns −0.602), that naive TWFE misses by multiples of the CS error, and that placebos on never-treated channels return ~0. The identical code path then runs on real data.
  • Cross-language check. make crosscheck runs Python's differences package against R's reference did implementation and requires agreement to machine precision. Enforced in CI.
  • Pipeline. src/{ingest,panel,estimate,diagnostics,report}/, one YAML config, a Makefile where every artifact has a rule and make all runs end-to-end with zero network. Dependencies pinned via uv; ruff, mypy and pytest with HTML fixtures for the scrapers.
  • Data provenance. The 2021-era channel roster comes from Wayback-archived rankings rather than today's top channels, which would have baked in survivorship bias. Prices sit in a curated table with a source URL on every row. Scraping respected robots.txt and rate limits; bot-protected pages were never circumvented by automation.
Step 5

The estimator was validated against a planted answer and a second implementation before it ever saw Twitch data. What remains is what the data itself cannot tell us.

06

The caveats

6 Where to read the result carefully

Where a flaw pushes the answer in a known direction, that direction is stated. Three of the six make the estimate too small, which is a different kind of problem from one that makes it too big.

  1. Only large channels are visible. Public trackers miss the long tail. Since the effect is concentrated in smaller channels, the platform-wide number is plausibly larger than ours. Bias: toward zero.
  2. The control is not perfectly untouched. English-language channels have non-US viewers who did get discounts, so the control group moved a little in the same direction as the treated. Bias: toward zero.
  3. Gift subscriptions are mixed in. Twitch reported roughly 5× gifting in Turkey and Mexico after the cuts. Part of what we measure is gifting behaviour, which has its own price sensitivity. Bias: direction unknown.
  4. Streaming was booming anyway. Spanish and Portuguese Twitch exploded in 2021 for reasons beyond price — the clearest threat to the whole design. Addressed by the Prime placebo, the trend sensitivity and the dose-response contrast, but not eliminated. Bias: likely upward.
  5. Some countries rest on few channels. Mexico's result comes from 2 channels, Argentina's from 4 — and Argentina's negative estimate partly reflects a top streamer stepping back in 2022. This is why the permutation test, not the standard error, carries the uncertainty.
  6. Currencies moved. The lira fell about 45% in late 2021. The dose is fixed at rollout-date exchange rates and the local-currency ratio is FX-free, but the discount viewers actually experienced kept drifting afterwards.

With Twitch's internal data most of this dissolves. Treatment could be assigned by each subscriber's actual billing country, which removes both the contaminated control and the Spanish-language assignment problem at once. The pre-rollout price tests in Brazil would supply randomized variation instead of a parallel-trends assumption. New subscriptions, renewals, gifts and Prime conversions could be separated, since pricing strategy needs to know which margin moved. And revenue per subscriber, net of tax and FX, would turn a single global elasticity into a revenue-maximising price per country.

Step 6

Treat this as a solid estimate for large creators, not the final word for the whole platform.
