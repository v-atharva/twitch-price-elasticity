A natural-experiment study · 2020–2022 panel

Did cheaper subs sell more subs?

In mid-2021, Twitch made channel subscriptions cheaper in nearly every country — 77% cheaper in Turkey, 20% cheaper in Western Europe — while US prices never moved. Different countries, different discounts, different dates, and one group that never changed: a natural experiment for the question every subscription business asks. When the price drops, how many more people subscribe?

01

The experiment Twitch ran for us

Before May 2021, subscribing to a channel cost about $4.99 everywhere on Earth. Then Twitch re-priced country by country to match local cost of living. Crucially for us, the discount depends on where the viewer lives — and the countries got their new prices on different dates, with different discounts. That staggered rollout is what lets us measure cause and effect instead of just correlation.

02

How the comparison works

A Turkish-language channel has mostly Turkish viewers, so it "gets" Turkey's price cut; a US-focused channel never gets one. We group channels by their audience's country (using broadcast language and where the streamer lives) and compare each treated group against US channels, before and after the price change. The method — difference-in-differences — is the standard tool for exactly this situation, and every estimate is independently reproduced with a second implementation to guard against software error.

Cheaper subs sold more subs. A 10% price cut bought roughly 9% more paid subscribers, and countries with deeper cuts saw bigger gains. The growth came mostly from mid-sized channels — the very biggest barely moved — which is exactly the tier of creators the policy was designed to help.

Cross-checked against Twitch's own books

More subscribers, but not more revenue

Our subscriber counts come from a public tracker. To make sure they reflect reality, we matched all 68 channels against Twitch's actual internal payout records from 2021 (handled privately, never published). Everything lined up — and it revealed the real economic story.

That last number is the whole story in one line: far more subscribers, each paying far less, adds up to about the same revenue. It's exactly what you'd expect from a big price cut — and it's almost certainly why Twitch paired the rollout with a 12-month revenue guarantee to protect creators through the transition.

04

Did it hold up?

A result is only as good as the attempts made to break it. Six checks, each of which could have killed this one:

The free-subs check
passed

Prime subs cost nothing, so a price cut shouldn't move them — and it didn't. An early version of the analysis failed this check, which exposed a flaw we then fixed. The final result passes cleanly.

The fluke check
1 in 200

We randomly shuffled which channels "got" the price cut, 200 times. Not one shuffle produced an effect as big as the real one — this is very unlikely to be luck.

The head-start check
passed

Treated channels weren't already pulling ahead before prices changed — the two groups moved in parallel. And pretending US channels were "treated" produces no effect at all.

The method check
−50% inflation

The simpler textbook approach overstates the effect by half. We diagnosed exactly why it goes wrong with staggered rollouts and used the modern estimator built for them.

The worst-case check
still positive

If we assume the small (statistically invisible) pre-existing drift had continued, the measured effect shrinks by about half — smaller, but still clearly there.

The dry-run check
exact recovery

Before touching real data, the entire pipeline ran on simulated data with a known planted answer — and recovered it. Two independent software implementations agree to the decimal.

05

Limitations & challenges

  1. Only large channels are visible. Public tracking data doesn't reach back far enough for smaller channels, so this is a study of 2021's top creators — the long tail may respond even more strongly. (These channels are, at least, confirmed genuine: all 68 appear in Twitch's own payout records.)
  2. The comparison group isn't perfectly untouched. US-focused channels have some international viewers who did get discounts. This biases our estimate down, not up.
  3. Gift subscriptions are mixed in. Twitch reported a surge of gifting after the cuts; part of the growth we measure is people gifting subs to others.
  4. Streaming was booming anyway. Spanish- and Portuguese-language Twitch exploded in 2021 for reasons beyond price. Our checks address this, but can't erase it entirely.
  5. Some countries rest on few channels. Mexico's result comes from 2 channels, Argentina's from 4 — individual results are noisy even where the overall pattern is clear.
  6. Currencies moved. Turkey's lira lost ~45% of its value soon after rollout, so the discount people actually experienced kept shifting.
06

What would make this better

Viewer-level treatment

Assign by each subscriber's billing country — control contamination and the language proxy vanish in one move.

Randomized price logs

Twitch ran pre-rollout price tests (Brazil). Experiment logs replace parallel-trends assumptions entirely.

Separate the margins

New vs renewal vs gift vs Prime conversion — counts hide which margin moves, and pricing strategy lives in that split.

Revenue, not volume

Per-country net revenue per sub (tax, FX, splits) → the revenue-maximizing price by country, not one global ε.

The long tail

Affordability effects are plausibly largest where trackers can't see; that's also where the revenue guarantee's cost/benefit is decided.

Spillovers

Gifts flow across borders and viewers migrate between channels — SUTVA violations only a full interaction graph can size.
