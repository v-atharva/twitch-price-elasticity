# What we found, in plain terms

In 2021, Twitch cut the price of channel subscriptions in most countries outside the US — by a lot where incomes are lower (about 77% in Turkey, 60% in Argentina, 52% in Mexico, 69% in Brazil) and a little in Western Europe (about 20%). US prices didn't change. Because different countries got different cuts at different times, this works like a natural experiment: we can compare subscriber growth in treated countries against similar US-focused channels that never got a price change.

**Cheaper subs sold more subs.** Across 29 large treated channels, paid subscriptions rose roughly 40–75% relative to US controls in the year after prices fell, and countries with bigger cuts saw bigger gains. The clearest single case: Turkey's top streamer went from ~900 paid subs in April 2021 to ~6,500 by October, while his *free* Prime subs — which the price cut shouldn't affect — actually declined.

**The price sensitivity we measure is about −0.9**: a 10% price cut buys roughly 9% more subscribers (plausible range, honestly wide with this sample: 3% to 14%). Taken at face value, that means the cuts roughly paid for themselves in volume where prices fell by half or more, but not from the biggest channels — the growth came disproportionately from the mid-sized creators in our sample, with the very largest showing little change. That pattern suggests affordability matters most below the superstar tier, which is exactly where Twitch aimed the policy.

**Honest caveats.** This is a study of large channels only (public tracker data doesn't cover smaller ones before 2021), gift subscriptions are mixed into the counts, and audience country is approximated by broadcast language. Statistical checks — placebo tests on free subs, fake treatment dates, permutation tests — support the finding, but the exact size of the effect carries real uncertainty.

*One line for a pricing discussion: demand responded strongly to local pricing, volume gains roughly offset ~half-price cuts, and the response concentrated below the top tier — where first-party data could measure it far better than we can from outside.*
